What this power calculator does

This tool covers both common forms of power in one page. The mechanical panel handles P = W / t — the rate at which work is done — with three solve modes for power, work or time, and unit-aware inputs (work in J or kJ, time in s, min, or h). The electrical panel handles P = V × I — the rate at which electrical energy is transferred — with its own three solve modes and unit-aware inputs (voltage in V or kV, current in A or mA). Every result includes step-by-step working, unit consistency checks, and real-world appliance comparisons so you can tell immediately whether a computed power value corresponds to an LED bulb, a ceiling fan, or a small car engine.

Formulas

Mechanical power
P = W t
Electrical power
P = V × I
Electrical power — resistive loads (Ohm's law substitution)
P = I² R   ·   P = V² R

Variable definitions

  • P — power, in watts (W). 1 W = 1 J/s = 1 V·A.
  • W — work or energy transferred, in joules (J).
  • t — time, in seconds (s).
  • V — voltage (potential difference), in volts (V).
  • I — current, in amperes (A).
  • R — resistance, in ohms (Ω), when the load is resistive.

Unit conversions you need to know

  • 1 kW = 1000 W
  • 1 hp (mechanical horsepower) ≈ 746 W
  • 1 kV = 1000 V
  • 1 mA = 0.001 A
  • 1 kWh = 3,600,000 J = 3.6 MJ

The two formulas describe the same physical quantity — the rate of energy transfer — but for different kinds of systems. Mechanical power applies to motors, engines, and any mechanical process. Electrical power applies to circuits, appliances, and electronic devices. In many real systems the two are linked: an electric motor consumes electrical power and delivers mechanical power, with the difference lost as heat.

Worked examples

Example 1: Find power — a labourer lifting bricks

W = 3000 J · t = 10 s
  1. P = W / t = 3000 / 10.
  2. P = 300 W ≈ 0.40 hp.
  3. Sustained 300 W of mechanical power is at the upper end of what a person can maintain.

Example 2: Find power — a small car engine

W = 7,500,000 J · t = 10 s
  1. P = 7,500,000 / 10.
  2. P = 750,000 W = 750 kW ≈ 1006 hp.
  3. A typical small car delivers about 75 kW. 750 kW is a sports/supercar range — 1000 hp is Bugatti territory.

Example 3: Find work from power and time

P = 1500 W · t = 3 min
  1. Convert time to seconds: 3 × 60 = 180 s.
  2. W = P × t = 1500 × 180.
  3. W = 270,000 J = 270 kJ.
  4. This is roughly the energy needed to boil 0.8 L of water — a kettle running for 3 minutes.

Example 4: Find time — a pump doing work

W = 900,000 J · P = 3000 W
  1. t = W / P = 900,000 / 3000.
  2. t = 300 s = 5 min.
  3. A 3 kW pump takes 5 minutes to perform 900 kJ of work — for example, lifting 15,000 L of water 6 m.

Example 5: Electrical — a household LED bulb

V = 220 V · I = 0.05 A
  1. P = V × I = 220 × 0.05.
  2. P = 11 W.
  3. An 11 W LED bulb produces the same light as an old 60 W filament bulb — about 5× more efficient.

Example 6: Electrical — an electric kettle

V = 220 V · I = 9 A
  1. P = 220 × 9.
  2. P = 1980 W ≈ 2 kW.
  3. Kettles and heaters are the largest household loads — 2 kW for 3 minutes uses 0.1 kWh, costing a few rupees.

Power in perspective — appliance and device scale

Because power is the rate at which energy is used, it determines how quickly an appliance drains energy — and how fast it delivers work. These reference points help you judge whether a computed value is reasonable.

  • ~0.001 W — a quartz watch.
  • ~0.1 W — a blinking LED indicator.
  • ~5–15 W — an LED bulb.
  • ~60–80 W — a ceiling fan.
  • ~100–200 W — a refrigerator (average).
  • ~150 W — a human being at rest (basal metabolic rate).
  • ~300–1000 W — a desktop computer or gaming PC.
  • ~800–1200 W — a microwave oven.
  • ~1500–3000 W — an electric kettle, iron, or space heater.
  • ~7500 W — a small car engine cruising (10 hp).
  • ~75,000 W — a small car engine at full throttle (100 hp).
  • ~10,000,000 W — a diesel locomotive.
  • ~100,000,000 W — a large jet engine.

Notice the 8 orders of magnitude between a blinking LED (0.1 W) and a jet engine (100 MW). The correct output unit (W, kW, hp, MW) matters as much as the number itself.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Confusing work and power. Work is total energy (joules); power is the rate (watts). A 100 J task done in 1 s is 100 W; the same 100 J done in 10 s is only 10 W. The work is the same; the power differs.
  • Using seconds when you mean hours (or vice versa). The mechanical formula P = W / t uses seconds in SI. If the time is given in minutes or hours, convert to seconds before dividing, or use the calculator's unit dropdown.
  • Mixing kW·h with W. Kilowatt-hours measure energy, not power. To convert to joules: 1 kWh = 3,600,000 J. Household electricity bills are in kWh, not kW.
  • Forgetting that V × I assumes DC or resistive AC loads. For AC circuits with motors or transformers, a power factor reduces the real power: P = V × I × cos φ. The simple V × I formula gives apparent power (VA), which is higher than real power (W).
  • Using horsepower and watts interchangeably. 1 hp ≈ 746 W. A 100 hp engine is about 75 kW, not 100 kW. Mixing the units by a factor of 0.75 gives answers that are subtly wrong.
  • Assuming power ratings on appliances are exact. A "1500 W" kettle draws close to 1500 W at the rated voltage, but at 210 V instead of 220 V it draws less. The nameplate is a nominal rating.
  • Ignoring efficiency. An electric motor rated at 2 kW input might deliver only 1.8 kW mechanical output — the remaining 200 W is lost as heat and sound. Input power ≠ output power.
  • Dropping the sign of negative power. In braking scenarios, mechanical power is negative — energy is being removed from the system. The magnitude is what matters for calculations, but the sign carries direction information.

FAQ

What is the formula for power?▾

Mechanical power is work divided by time: P = W / t. The SI unit is the watt (W), where 1 W = 1 joule per second. Electrical power is voltage times current: P = V × I, also in watts. Both formulas describe the same physical quantity — the rate of energy transfer.

What is the difference between work and power?▾

Work is the total energy transferred, measured in joules. Power is the rate at which that energy is transferred, measured in watts. A 100 J task done in 2 seconds uses 50 W of power; the same 100 J done in 20 seconds uses only 5 W. The work is identical, but the power differs by a factor of 10.

What is horsepower?▾

Horsepower (hp) is an older unit of power, originally based on the work a horse could do. One mechanical horsepower equals about 746 watts. Car engines, pumps and motors are often rated in hp — for example, a typical small car engine produces 100 hp, or about 75 kW.

How much power does a typical household appliance use?▾

An LED bulb uses 5 to 15 W, a ceiling fan uses 60 to 80 W, a refrigerator uses 100 to 200 W, a microwave uses 800 to 1200 W, an air conditioner uses 1000 to 2000 W, and an electric kettle uses 1500 to 3000 W. This is why appliances with heating elements (kettles, heaters, irons) are the largest loads in a home.

How do I calculate my electricity bill from power?▾

Multiply the appliance's power (in kW) by the hours it runs each day, and by the number of days. That gives the energy in kilowatt-hours (kWh). Multiply by your electricity rate per kWh to get the cost. For example, a 1500 W kettle used 10 minutes a day uses 1500/1000 × (10/60) = 0.25 kWh per day.

Why is power negative in some cases?▾

Negative power means energy is being removed from the system rather than added. For example, a car braking at constant deceleration has negative mechanical power — the brakes are converting kinetic energy into heat at a certain rate. In the formula P = W/t, a negative work input over a positive time gives negative power.
