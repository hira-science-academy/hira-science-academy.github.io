What this cumulative GPA calculator does

Your cumulative GPA (often written CGPA) is the credit-weighted average of all the grades you have earned so far. This calculator has two modes. Update my GPA combines your current GPA and credits with a new semester's GPA and credits to give your new cumulative GPA. Find the GPA I need works backwards: enter your current record, the credits you will take next and a target, and it tells you the semester GPA you need, or whether the target is out of reach. Every result shows the full arithmetic and whether your GPA is rising, falling or staying level.

Cumulative GPA formulas

New cumulative GPA
New GPA = (old GPA × old credits) + (term GPA × term credits) old credits + term credits
Semester GPA needed for a target
Needed GPA = target × (old credits + term credits) − (old GPA × old credits) term credits

Variable definitions

  • Old GPA: your cumulative GPA before the new term.
  • Old credits: credits counted in your GPA so far (credits attempted for a grade).
  • Term GPA and term credits: the new semester's GPA and its credits.
  • Target: the cumulative GPA you want to reach.

Worked examples

Example 1: GPA goes up

Current GPA 3.20 over 60 credits. New semester: 3.75 GPA over 15 credits.
  1. Old quality points: 3.20 × 60 = 192.
  2. New quality points: 3.75 × 15 = 56.25.
  3. Total: 192 + 56.25 = 248.25 over 60 + 15 = 75 credits.
  4. New GPA = 248.25 ÷ 75 = 3.31, up 0.11.

Example 2: GPA goes down

Current GPA 3.50 over 60 credits. New semester: 3.00 GPA over 15 credits.
  1. 3.50 × 60 = 210 and 3.00 × 15 = 45.
  2. (210 + 45) ÷ 75 = 255 ÷ 75 = 3.40, down 0.10.
  3. The GPA fell by only 0.10 even though the semester was 0.50 below the old GPA, because the new term is just 15 of 75 credits.

Example 3: GPA stays the same

Current GPA 3.25 over 45 credits. New semester: 3.25 over 15 credits.
  1. 3.25 × 60 = 195, and 195 ÷ 60 = 3.25.
  2. When the new term equals your cumulative GPA, the average does not move.

Example 4: What GPA do I need to reach a 3.20?

Current GPA 3.10 over 60 credits. Next term: 15 credits. Target: 3.20.
  1. Quality points needed: 3.20 × 75 = 240.
  2. Current quality points: 3.10 × 60 = 186.
  3. Needed next term: 240 − 186 = 54, and 54 ÷ 15 = 3.60.
  4. You need a 3.60 semester GPA to reach a 3.20 cumulative GPA.

Example 5: Getting a 2.80 up to a 3.00 for a scholarship

Current GPA 2.80 over 45 credits. Next term: 15 credits. Target: 3.00.
  1. Needed total: 3.00 × 60 = 180. Current: 2.80 × 45 = 126.
  2. Needed next term: 180 − 126 = 54, and 54 ÷ 15 = 3.60.
  3. A 3.60 term would lift a 2.80 to a 3.00.

Example 6: How low can a strong GPA drop?

Current GPA 3.80 over 90 credits. Next term: 15 credits. Target: stay at 3.50 or higher.
  1. Needed total: 3.50 × 105 = 367.5. Current: 3.80 × 90 = 342.
  2. Needed next term: 367.5 − 342 = 25.5, and 25.5 ÷ 15 = 1.70.
  3. You could earn as low as a 1.70 term and still finish at 3.50.

Example 7: A target that is out of reach

Current GPA 2.20 over 90 credits. Next term: 12 credits. Target: 3.00 on a 4.0 scale.
  1. Needed total: 3.00 × 102 = 306. Current: 2.20 × 90 = 198.
  2. Needed next term: 306 − 198 = 108, and 108 ÷ 12 = 9.00, far above 4.0.
  3. Not reachable in one term. With straight 4.0 terms you would need about (3.00 × 90 − 198) ÷ (4.0 − 3.00) = 72 more credits, roughly five 15-credit semesters.

How credit weighting works

Credits act as weights, so the more credits you have, the less one semester can move your cumulative GPA. Compare two students who each earn a 4.0 term of 15 credits while sitting on a 3.0:

  • Early (30 credits so far): the new term is one third of the total, so the GPA rises by about 0.33, to 3.33.
  • Late (105 credits so far): the new term is about one eighth of the total, so the GPA rises by only 0.125, to 3.125.

This is why it is easiest to change your GPA early in a degree, and why a bad first year is harder to repair later.

What is a good cumulative GPA in the US?

These are typical benchmarks on a 4.0 scale. Each school sets its own rules, so check your catalog.

Cumulative GPATypical meaning
2.0C average. Usually the minimum for good academic standing and to graduate.
3.0B average. A common minimum for scholarships, many graduate programs and internships.
3.5Often the dean's list level and a common cum laude cutoff.
3.7Often the magna cum laude level.
3.9Often the summa cum laude level at schools that use GPA cutoffs.

Using this calculator in the UK and Canada

UK: most universities average your module marks, weighted by credits, rather than using GPA. You can use the Percentage scale here to get a credit-weighted average mark. Degree classes are usually based on that average, typically First at 70% and above, 2:1 at 60–69%, 2:2 at 50–59% and Third at 40–49%, though each university has its own rules.

Canada: many universities use a 4.0 scale, some use 4.3 or other scales, and others report percentages. Choose the scale that matches your transcript and use the same one for every entry.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Averaging two GPAs directly. (old GPA + new GPA) ÷ 2 is only right when the credits are equal. Weight by credits.
  • Using credits earned instead of credits attempted. Most schools compute GPA from credits attempted for a grade, so a failed course still counts. Pass/fail and transfer credits are usually left out.
  • Entering last semester's GPA as your cumulative GPA. The first field is your running average from the transcript.
  • Forgetting the new credits in the total. The denominator is old credits plus new credits.
  • Mixing scales. Do not enter a 4.3 or a weighted 5.0 value on a 4.0 scale. Use one scale throughout.
  • Ignoring your school's repeat-course policy. Some schools replace the old grade and others average both.
  • Rounding too early. Keep full precision and round only the final GPA to two decimals.
  • Assuming any target is reachable. If the needed GPA is above the scale maximum, the target cannot be reached in one term. This calculator tells you how many more credits it would take.

FAQ

How do I calculate my cumulative GPA after a new semester?▾

Multiply your old GPA by your old credits, add the new semester's GPA times its credits, and divide by the total credits: (old GPA × old credits + term GPA × term credits) ÷ (old credits + term credits). For example, (3.20 × 60 + 3.75 × 15) ÷ 75 = 3.31.

What is the difference between GPA and CGPA?▾

GPA usually means the average for one term, while CGPA (cumulative GPA) is the average across every term you have completed. In the US it is simply called your cumulative GPA, and it is the figure shown on your transcript.

Can my cumulative GPA go down?▾

Yes. If a semester's GPA is lower than your current cumulative GPA, the cumulative GPA falls. If it is higher, the cumulative GPA rises. The more credits you already have, the smaller the change.

How much does one semester change my cumulative GPA?▾

It depends on the share of total credits the new term makes up. A 15-credit term on top of 100 credits is about 13% of the total, while the same term on top of 30 credits is one third, so it has much more influence.

What GPA do I need next semester to reach my target?▾

Use the formula (target × total credits after the term − old GPA × old credits) ÷ term credits, or switch this calculator to "Find the GPA I need". If the result is above the scale maximum, the target cannot be reached in one term.

What is a good cumulative GPA?▾

A 2.0 is the usual minimum for good standing and graduation, 3.0 is a B average and a common scholarship or graduate school threshold, and 3.5 or higher is often dean's list or cum laude level. Each school sets its own cutoffs.

Which credits count toward my GPA?▾

Most schools count credits attempted for a letter grade, including courses you failed. Pass/fail courses, withdrawals and many transfer credits are usually excluded. Check your school's policy and use the credits shown as GPA hours on your transcript.

How does repeating a course affect my cumulative GPA?▾

Policies differ. Some schools replace the old grade with the new one, while others average both attempts. Enter your courses the way your school counts them, and ask your registrar if you are unsure.

Can I use this calculator in the UK or Canada?▾

Yes. In Canada, choose the scale on your transcript (4.0 or 4.3 are common). In the UK, choose the Percentage scale to get a credit-weighted average mark, which is what most universities use to decide degree classes.
