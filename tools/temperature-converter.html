What this temperature converter does

This converter changes temperatures between Celsius (°C), Fahrenheit (°F) and Kelvin (K). Use it for weather forecasts, recipes, body temperature, science homework and travel. In the US, everyday temperatures are given in Fahrenheit, while the UK and Canada use Celsius for weather. Recipes vary: UK recipes use °C and gas marks, and many Canadian recipes list oven temperatures in °F. The converter shows the answer on all three scales, explains the formula step by step, and rejects any input below absolute zero.

Temperature conversion formulas

Celsius to Fahrenheit and back
°F = (°C × 95 ) + 32
°C = (°F − 32) × 59
Celsius to Kelvin and back
K = °C + 273.15
°C = K − 273.15
Fahrenheit to Kelvin and back
K = (°F + 459.67) × 59
°F = (K × 95 ) − 459.67

A quick mental shortcut

For everyday temperatures, double the Celsius value and add 30 to estimate Fahrenheit. For example, 20 °C is about 70 °F (the exact answer is 68 °F). To go the other way, subtract 30 from Fahrenheit and halve it. The shortcut is close between about 0 °C and 30 °C and gets less accurate outside that range.

Why the offset matters

Water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C. On the Fahrenheit scale the same two points are 32 °F and 212 °F, so there are 180 Fahrenheit degrees for every 100 Celsius degrees, a ratio of 9 to 5. Because the zero points also differ, the 32 appears when you convert an absolute temperature. When you convert a change in temperature, such as "a 10 °C rise", you multiply by 9/5 and do not add 32.

Worked examples

Example 1: 72 °F to °C (a US thermostat)

  1. °C = (72 − 32) × 5/9.
  2. 40 × 5/9 = 22.22.
  3. 72 °F = 22.2 °C, a comfortable room temperature.

Example 2: 350 °F to °C (a baking temperature)

  1. °C = (350 − 32) × 5/9.
  2. 318 × 5/9 = 176.67.
  3. 350 °F = 176.7 °C. UK and Canadian recipes usually round this to 180 °C, which is Gas Mark 4.

Example 3: 20 °C to °F (a mild UK day)

  1. °F = (20 × 9/5) + 32.
  2. 20 × 1.8 = 36, and 36 + 32 = 68.
  3. 20 °C = 68 °F.

Example 4: 100.4 °F to °C (fever threshold)

  1. °C = (100.4 − 32) × 5/9.
  2. 68.4 × 5/9 = 38.
  3. 100.4 °F = 38 °C, the level health agencies commonly treat as a fever.

Example 5: −10 °C to °F (a Canadian winter day)

  1. °F = (−10 × 9/5) + 32.
  2. −10 × 1.8 = −18, and −18 + 32 = 14.
  3. −10 °C = 14 °F.

Example 6: 165 °F to °C (safe poultry temperature)

  1. °C = (165 − 32) × 5/9.
  2. 133 × 5/9 = 73.89.
  3. 165 °F = 73.9 °C, the USDA safe internal temperature for poultry.

Example 7: Celsius to Kelvin

  1. 100 °C: K = 100 + 273.15 = 373.15 K (water boils at sea level).
  2. 300 K: °C = 300 − 273.15 = 26.85 °C (a warm room in many physics problems).
  3. 0 K: °C = 0 − 273.15 = −273.15 °C, absolute zero.

Example 8: The point where the scales match

  1. °F = (−40 × 9/5) + 32 = −72 + 32.
  2. −40 °C = −40 °F. This is the only temperature that is the same on both scales.

Why Kelvin matters in science

Kelvin is the SI base unit of temperature because it starts at absolute zero. That makes it the right scale for physics and chemistry:

  • Kelvin is proportional to average molecular kinetic energy. Doubling the Kelvin temperature doubles the average kinetic energy. Celsius and Fahrenheit do not have this property.
  • Gas laws need Kelvin. In the ideal gas law PV = nRT, T must be in kelvin. Using Celsius gives a wrong answer.
  • Absolute zero is only meaningful in Kelvin. At 0 K molecular motion is at its minimum, while at 0 °C water is just freezing.

Write kelvin without a degree symbol: "300 K", not "300 °K".

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Forgetting the 32. °C × 9/5 alone gives the right answer only at −40, where the two scales meet.
  • Adding 32 to a temperature change. A 10 °C rise is an 18 °F rise, not 50 °F. Intervals use only the 9/5 factor.
  • Writing "°K". Kelvin has no degree symbol.
  • Using Celsius in gas-law formulas. PV = nRT needs kelvin.
  • Mixing up hot days. 100 °F is a very hot day (37.8 °C), while 100 °C is boiling water.
  • Assuming 1 °C equals 1 °F. One Celsius degree is 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees, so a 5 °C change is a 9 °F change.
  • Going below absolute zero. Nothing can be colder than 0 K (−273.15 °C or −459.67 °F), so the converter rejects those inputs.

FAQ

How do I convert Celsius to Fahrenheit?▾

Multiply the Celsius temperature by 9/5 (or 1.8) and add 32: °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32. For example, 37 °C = (37 × 1.8) + 32 = 98.6 °F. For a quick estimate, double the Celsius value and add 30.

How do I convert Fahrenheit to Celsius?▾

Subtract 32 from the Fahrenheit temperature and multiply by 5/9: °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9. For example, 72 °F = (72 − 32) × 5/9 = 22.2 °C. For a quick estimate, subtract 30 and halve it.

What is 350 °F in Celsius?▾

350 °F is about 176.7 °C. Recipes usually round it to 180 °C, which is Gas Mark 4 in the UK. Use the oven chart on this page for other common settings.

What is normal body temperature, and what counts as a fever?▾

Normal body temperature is about 37 °C (98.6 °F), although it varies by person and time of day. Health agencies such as the CDC commonly define a fever as 100.4 °F (38 °C) or higher. This is general information, not medical advice.

Which countries use Fahrenheit and which use Celsius?▾

The United States uses Fahrenheit for everyday temperatures, as do a few places such as the Bahamas, Belize and the Cayman Islands. The UK and Canada use Celsius for weather. UK recipes use °C and gas marks, while many Canadian recipes list oven temperatures in °F.

How do I convert Celsius to Kelvin?▾

Add 273.15: K = °C + 273.15. For example, 25 °C = 298.15 K. To go back, subtract 273.15. Kelvin is the SI base unit of temperature and is written without a degree symbol, as in 298 K.

What is absolute zero?▾

Absolute zero is the lowest possible temperature, 0 K, which equals −273.15 °C or −459.67 °F. Nothing can be colder, so this converter rejects any input below it.

Why are −40 °C and −40 °F the same?▾

The two scales are linear with different zero points and degree sizes, so their lines cross at exactly one point. Setting °F = °C in the conversion formula gives −40.

Is a 1 °C change the same as a 1 °F change?▾

No. One Celsius degree is 1.8 times larger than one Fahrenheit degree, so a 1 °C change equals a 1.8 °F change. Temperature differences use only the 9/5 factor, and the offset of 32 applies only to absolute temperatures.
