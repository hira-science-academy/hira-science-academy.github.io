Why More Than Half of Punjab's Class 9 Students Failed Mathematics in 2026

A teacher's analysis of what went wrong — and what parents and students can actually do about it

Published: September 27, 2026

With official pass percentage data from the BISE Gujranwala 9th Class Result Gazette 2026

Muhammad Tayyab

📑 Jump to: 📊 The Numbers 🧠 Memorisation 👩‍🏫 Teacher Shortages 🔄 Promotion Trap 📘 PECTAA ✅ What You Can Do

The 2026 BISE Punjab Class 9 results are out. And the mathematics result is, once again, the lowest-performing subject across all 9 Punjab boards — with fewer than half of the students who appeared actually passing the exam.

I teach Class 9 and Class 10 Mathematics in Daska. I've seen these numbers arrive every year, and every year the newspapers give the same explanation: "students didn't work hard enough" or "the paper was difficult this year."

That explanation is not honest. Students in Punjab work extremely hard. The problem is not effort — it's the specific structure of how Class 9 mathematics is taught, examined, and supported in our schools. Let me explain what I actually see in the classroom, and what the official gazette numbers actually say.

The Numbers, First — From the Official Gazette

The BISE Gujranwala 2026 result gazette gives us the clearest picture. Out of 249,352 students who appeared for the 9th Class First Annual Examination, only 116,340 passed — an overall pass percentage of just 46.66%.

The Science Group (which includes Mathematics as a compulsory subject) had a pass percentage of 54.43%. That means nearly half of all Science Group students failed at least one subject — and Mathematics is almost always the culprit.

The General Group was even worse: only 28.99% passed. And the pass percentage has been hovering between 44% and 54% for the last five years. This is not a one-year anomaly. It is a systemic pattern.

Reason 1: Mathematics Is Taught as Memorisation, Not Thinking

Walk into most Class 9 mathematics classrooms in Punjab, and you'll see the same scene: students copying down solutions from the board, memorising them, and reproducing them in the exam. The formulas are learned. The steps are learned. But the reasoning behind the steps is never discussed.

Here's what happens in the exam hall. A question on factorization appears. The student has memorised four or five methods, but the specific question doesn't match any pattern they've seen. They freeze. They try the wrong method. They run out of time.

This is not a student failure — it's a teaching failure. A student who understands why the method works can adapt to any question. A student who has only memorised the method cannot.

In my own classroom, I now start every new topic by showing students the wrong approach first. I solve a problem incorrectly and ask them to spot the mistake. Then I solve it correctly. In my observation, students who see the failure first remember the correct method far better than students who only see the correct method.

Reason 2: Teacher Shortages and Classroom Overload

In many government schools, one mathematics teacher is responsible for more than 100 students across multiple sections. Under those conditions, no teacher — no matter how skilled — can give individual attention to each student's mathematical reasoning.

In the schools I've taught in across Daska, I've seen mathematics teachers take on 4–5 classes per day, plus administrative duties, leaving almost no time for the individual follow-up that struggling students need.

The result is that the students who fall behind in September are never caught up. By the time the exam arrives in March, they have gone seven months without understanding half the syllabus — and there is nothing anyone can do about it at that point.

Reason 3: Automatic Promotion Creates Gaps That Compound

Under Punjab's current promotion policy, students who fail Class 8 are often promoted to Class 9 anyway. That seems compassionate on the surface. But in practice, it means a student who never properly learned Grade 7 fractions arrives in a Class 9 classroom that expects them to handle algebraic rational expressions.

You cannot teach a student to solve quadratic equations if they still cannot simplify a fraction. They will memorise the quadratic formula — because they have to — but they will not be able to apply it correctly, because the foundation is missing.

Every year, in the first week of Class 9, I give students a basic diagnostic test covering Grade 7 and 8 arithmetic. In my experience, roughly 60–70% of students cannot pass it. That is the real starting point. And it is not their fault.

Reason 4: PECTAA's New Curriculum Is Concept-Heavy but Support Is Thin

The PECTAA 2026 syllabus is genuinely better than the previous one — it emphasises understanding over rote memorisation, which is the correct direction. But curriculum reform without corresponding teacher training and resource development creates a mismatch.

The new exercises require students to explain why a mathematical step works. Many Class 9 teachers, through no fault of their own, have never been trained to teach that way. They were trained in the old system and taught in the old system. They are now expected to teach a fundamentally different approach — with no additional support.

This is not a critique of teachers. It's a critique of the pace of reform without the resources to match.

What Parents and Students Can Actually Do

I'm not in a position to reform the Punjab education system. But I can tell you what works for the students who pass with good marks — even in the current system.

1. Focus on the "why" before the "how"

When your child is doing a mathematical exercise, ask them to explain in their own words why the method works. If they can't, they've memorised it, not learned it. Go back and understand the underlying concept before moving to the next exercise.

2. Fill gaps from earlier years first

If a Class 9 student is struggling, the problem is often not in Class 9 — it's in Class 7 or 8. Spend two weeks re-learning Grade 7 algebra and Grade 8 geometry. The Class 9 topics will then click into place much faster.

3. Use the pairing scheme to prioritise

Not every chapter carries equal weight. The 2027 pairing scheme shows exactly how many marks each chapter contributes to the final exam. Students should spend proportional time on high-weight chapters first, then cover the rest.

4. Solve past papers under timed conditions

A student who has never solved a full past paper in 2 hours and 30 minutes will freeze in the real exam. Practice the actual format. Time yourself. Identify where you slow down and why.

Final Thought

The 2026 Class 9 mathematics results are a symptom, not a disease. The disease is a system that rewards memorisation over understanding, and that offers too little support to both students and teachers. Until that changes, the results will not.

But the individual student can still succeed. Mathematics is not a talent — it's a skill. A student who understands why before memorising what will outperform a student who does it the other way around. Every single time.

If you're a Class 9 student reading this, don't panic about the statistics. Understand the concepts. Fill the gaps. Practice the actual paper. And use the resources below.
